The forest in the car park

A planting method from 1970s Japan is turning strips of tarmac into dense young woods. What can a forest the size of a tennis court really do?

By Ada Lindgren · Photographs by Tomas Rey · 23 September 2026

On a Saturday in March, forty volunteers pulled up the asphalt behind a supermarket on the edge of the city. By the evening they had planted six hundred saplings in a plot not much larger than a tennis court: oak, rowan, hazel, wild cherry and a dozen other native species, packed so tightly that you could not walk between them.

Three summers later the plot is taller than the fence around it. Blackbirds nest in it. On hot afternoons the air on its shaded side is noticeably cooler, and the delivery drivers who park beside it have started to eat their lunch there.

A strip of tarmac

The site had been a car park overflow for twenty years. Rainwater ran off it into a storm drain, and in August the surface reached temperatures that made the neighbouring flats uncomfortable at night. When the supermarket reduced its parking, the council offered the strip to a residents' group on the condition that it would not become another lawn to mow.

The group chose a method they had read about online: dense, multi-layered planting of native species, designed to reach something like a mature woodland structure within a few decades instead of a century.

“We did not want a park. We wanted something that would look after itself once we stopped looking after it.”Mara Holt, residents' group coordinator

The Miyawaki method

The approach is named after the botanist Akira Miyawaki, who spent decades studying the remnant forests that survive around shrines and temples in Japan. He argued that those small patches showed what the land would grow if left alone, and that planting the same species, densely and all at once, could shortcut the slow process of natural succession.

In practice that means three to five saplings per square metre, a thick layer of mulch, and a deep preparation of the soil. For the first two or three years the plot is weeded and watered. After that, the idea is that it is left entirely alone.

What the numbers say

Supporters often claim that these forests grow ten times faster and become thirty times denser than conventional plantations. Those figures come mostly from early projects and from the method's own advocates, and independent studies have been more cautious.

What independent monitoring does tend to confirm is more modest and still useful: fast early height growth, high survival where the soil is well prepared, and a clear local cooling effect on the shaded side of a plot during heat waves.

What a small plot can do

  1. Shade and cool the ground and air close to it.
  2. Soak up rain that would otherwise run straight into drains.
  3. Give birds and insects a dense, sheltered patch in a hard landscape.
  4. Give neighbours a reason to meet, plant and argue about trees.

The honest limits

A forest the size of a tennis court will not offset a city's emissions. The carbon it stores over its lifetime is small next to a single year of a household's energy use, and ecologists warn against treating tiny forests as a substitute for protecting large, old woodland.

There are practical risks too. Dense planting on poorly prepared ground can fail, and a plot that is abandoned in its second summer drought may lose most of its saplings.

Planting your own

The residents' group has published its notes for anyone who wants to try. Their advice is unglamorous: start with the soil, choose species that already grow within a few kilometres, plant in the dormant season, and line up volunteers for watering before the first summer, not during it.

And be patient with the neighbours. The first year, their plot looked like a muddy rectangle of sticks. It was only in the third summer, when the canopy closed and the blackbirds arrived, that people stopped asking when the real trees would be planted.

  1. Miyawaki's surveys of shrine forests began in the 1970s and shaped his idea of "potential natural vegetation".
  2. Figures vary widely between sites. Independent studies stress soil preparation and aftercare as the main drivers of success.
  3. Small urban forests are best seen as local climate and biodiversity measures, not carbon offsets.
